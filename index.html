About the workshop

Generative AI is changing not only how students complete assessment, but also what assessment can meaningfully tell us about learning.

When students can use large language models and increasingly capable AI agents to write, analyse, code, plan, revise, and reason, a polished submission no longer provides straightforward evidence of what the student themselves understands or can do. At the same time, simply excluding AI is neither always feasible nor educationally desirable: effective use, critical evaluation, and oversight of AI are becoming important capabilities.

This workshop asks a central question: how should we design assessment in the age of AI?

Educational assessment traditionally assumes a relationship between an observable performance and an underlying student capability:

Assessment task → Student performance → Evidence of capability

Generative AI weakens this relationship. A report, program, design, analysis, or presentation may increasingly reflect the combined capabilities of a student, an LLM, an agent, external tools, and information resources. The resulting challenge is broader than academic-integrity detection:

What capability are we trying to assess, and what evidence can validly demonstrate that capability when AI is available?

The half-day program combines invited talks, assessment case studies, a technical forum, and a scenario-based panel. Its aim is to connect AI researchers, educators, learning scientists, and assessment specialists around a practical and researchable question: what counts as evidence of learning when AI can increasingly perform the task?

Three perspectives

Assessment under AI

If AI can reliably perform a task that previously provided evidence of learning, educators need to reconsider what the task actually measures. The answer need not be simply to prohibit AI or replace all assessment with examinations. Instead, assessment may need richer evidence:

  • Interactive oral assessment, where students explain, apply, and defend their reasoning in responsive dialogue;
  • Interactive or public presentations, where students repeatedly explain and respond to questions rather than deliver a rehearsed slide deck;
  • Authentic assessment, where students use contemporary disciplinary tools to solve realistic problems;
  • Longitudinal assessment, where evidence is collected across connected stages rather than only through a final product;
  • Process evidence, such as decisions, revisions, intermediate artefacts, and responses to feedback; and
  • Independent demonstrations, where particular foundational capabilities still need to be demonstrated without AI.

The aim is not simply to make assessment "AI-proof", but to determine what evidence is required to support valid conclusions about student capability.

Assessment with AI

AI may also become part of what assessment is intended to measure. In many professional settings, competent performance increasingly involves deciding what to delegate to AI, evaluating whether its output is appropriate, identifying missing information or assumptions, verifying claims, comparing alternatives, correcting errors, and remaining accountable for the final outcome. Assessment therefore may need to evaluate both independent human capability and effective human-AI capability.

The assessment question changes from Can the student produce this artefact? to Can the student work competently with AI, evaluate its outputs, improve them where necessary, and remain responsible for the final judgement?

AI for assessment

The technologies disrupting conventional assessment may also help educators improve it. AI tools and agents can potentially support the assessment lifecycle:

Design → Stress-test → Collect evidence → Question → Feedback → Moderate → Improve

For example, AI could test whether an assessment aligns with learning outcomes; attempt a task to expose where existing requirements can be trivially satisfied by AI; identify weak or ambiguous rubric criteria; generate personalised follow-up questions from student work; locate evidence against individual rubric criteria; support formative feedback; assist moderation across assessors; and identify uncertain cases for human review.

Agentic systems are particularly relevant because they can perform multi-step workflows involving retrieval, analysis, tool use, questioning, checking, and escalation rather than producing only a single response. This creates new AI research problems involving reliability, grounding, uncertainty, fairness, consistency, transparency, privacy, contestability, and human oversight — connecting directly to core AJCAI areas including generative AI, AI agents, NLP, multimodal reasoning, human-AI interaction, trustworthy and explainable AI, evaluation, and decision support.

Key questions

1. What capability should we assess?

  • Which capabilities remain important when AI can perform many component tasks?
  • What should students still demonstrate independently?
  • When should effective AI use itself become a learning outcome?
  • How should foundational competence and AI-augmented competence relate?

2. What counts as evidence?

  • When is a final product insufficient evidence of learning?
  • What can dialogue, process, revision, performance, or defence reveal?
  • How can assessment distinguish student capability from AI capability?
  • How can richer evidence remain scalable?

3. How should assessment be designed?

  • Interactive oral assessment; personalised viva and follow-up questioning;
  • Conference-style or poster-based presentations;
  • Authentic disciplinary assessment; staged and longitudinal assessment;
  • Process portfolios and revision histories;
  • Critique and verification of AI-generated work; and assessments in which AI use is explicitly required.

4. How can AI help educators assess?

  • Assessment-design and stress-testing agents; personalised question generation;
  • AI-assisted interactive oral assessment;
  • Evidence extraction from reports, code, presentations, and multimodal artefacts;
  • Rubric-grounded feedback; moderation and consistency checking;
  • Uncertainty-aware escalation; formative AI practice environments; and human-AI interfaces for assessment decisions.

Across all four areas, the workshop will consider validity, reliability, fairness, accessibility, scalability, workload, transparency, and human accountability.

Design principles

Technical forum

Designing and Scaling Assessment with AI

Short invited case presentations will ground the workshop in implemented assessment approaches rather than abstract discussion. Representative cases include:

Interactive oral assessment

Students participate in structured but responsive conversations in which assessors probe understanding, application, reasoning, and ownership. Discussion will consider both its evidentiary value and challenges of consistency and scalability.

Interactive or conference-style presentation

Poster, conference, or demonstration formats replace rehearsed slide presentations with repeated explanation, questioning, interaction, and defence.

Authentic technology-enabled assessment

Students use contemporary disciplinary technologies to perform realistic professional or research tasks. The relevant question becomes not whether technology should be removed, but what human capability needs to be demonstrated while using it.

Assessment as a learning journey

Evidence is collected through connected stages, enabling assessment of development, feedback uptake, decisions, and increasing independence.

AI-supported formative assessment

AI provides scalable practice, question generation, explanations, or feedback, raising questions about how formative AI support should connect to subsequent evidence of learning.

Structured panel

What counts as evidence of learning when AI can do the task?

The panel will use concrete scenarios rather than general opening statements.

  1. The AI-generated essay

    AI can produce an excellent conventional essay. Should the essay be retained, redesigned, combined with other evidence, or replaced?

  2. The AI-built project

    A student uses an AI agent for substantial parts of a software, engineering, or project-management task but can explain, verify, and defend every major decision. What capability has the student demonstrated?

  3. The scalable interactive oral

    Interactive oral assessment provides strong evidence but the course has 800 students. Where could AI help without weakening validity?

  4. AI use is required

    Students must use AI. Should the rubric assess prompting, verification, critique, revision, judgement, output quality, or accountability?

  5. AI assists the assessor

    An AI agent identifies evidence, proposes questions, and drafts feedback. Which activities can appropriately be delegated and which must remain under educator control?

Each scenario will be discussed through four dimensions:

Capability → Evidence → AI role → Human responsibility

Audience polling and short interventions will be used where feasible to surface differences across technical, educational, and policy perspectives.

Dr Zhigang Lu Confirmed

Western Sydney University

Beyond AI detection: Assessing Student Understanding in the Age of Generative AI (a real-time demo will be demonstrated in the talk).

The rapid adoption of generative AI is challenging traditional approaches to academic integrity, particularly for programming and other assignment-based assessments. Existing AI-detection methods attempt to determine whether a submitted artefact was generated by AI, but such detection is inherently fragile: generative models evolve rapidly, and today's detection techniques may not remain effective against tomorrow's models. This talk presents an alternative perspective: rather than attempting to detect AI, we assess the student's understanding of their own submitted work — a GenAI-assisted assessment system in which a locally deployed large language model reads and analyses each student's submission, automatically generates personalised questions about its design, implementation, and reasoning, and evaluates the student's responses under controlled conditions. The talk discusses the motivation, system design, practical challenges, and broader implications of this approach.

Dr Zhigang Lu is a Senior Lecturer at Western Sydney University, Australia. His research focuses on privacy and security in machine learning, with particular interests in differential privacy, privacy-preserving data analytics, and trustworthy generative AI, published at venues including USENIX Security, NDSS, ACM CCS, PETS, NeurIPS, and AAAI. He serves as an Associate Editor for IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing and an Area Consulting Editor for IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security.

Who should attend

We expect approximately 10–30 participants, including:

  • Researchers in generative AI, NLP, autonomous agents, multimodal AI, human-AI interaction, and trustworthy AI;
  • Researchers in AI in education, educational data mining, learning analytics, and educational assessment;
  • Educators and academic developers redesigning assessment.

The workshop is deliberately positioned at the interface between AI capability and assessment design. Technical researchers will encounter concrete problems requiring new AI methods, while educators and assessment researchers will encounter AI capabilities that may enable new assessment approaches rather than merely threaten existing ones.

Shared framework

The workshop aims to produce a concise shared framework:

  • Capability — What are we trying to assess?
  • Evidence — What observable evidence supports that conclusion?
  • Assessment design — What format generates that evidence?
  • AI role — Should AI be restricted, permitted, required, or used by the assessor?
  • Human responsibility — Which decisions must remain under meaningful human control?

Potential follow-up outputs include a synthesis of assessment-design principles, a research agenda for AI-supported assessment, candidate benchmark problems, and future cross-institutional collaborations.

Diversity, equity, and inclusion

The workshop will seek diversity in gender, career stage, discipline, institution, and geography across organisers, invited speakers, case presenters, and panellists. The program will also explicitly consider whether emerging AI-enabled assessment formats create new barriers through accessibility, language expectations, technology access, oral interaction, or algorithmic bias, and how these can be addressed through assessment.
